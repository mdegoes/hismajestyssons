# Voice and theology

His Majesty's Sons exists to equip men to live as kings under The King. Its songs
are meant for men to sing: in the pew, at the table, and in the field.

## Masculine register

Masculinity here comes from **stance**, not from volume or violence. God is
sovereign, Scripture is solid ground, and a man's answer is duty, oath, and worship.

| Soft (cut) | Why it fails | Strong (reach for) |
|---|---|---|
| "You made me whole" / "make me whole" | Therapy language: God as healer of the self | "Christ, the Word made flesh, is Lord!" (confession) |
| "I run to You" | Romance-film language: the soul as pursuer | "I will march at Your command" (a soldier's answer) |
| "calls me home" | Sentimental | "is the Word, and it will hold" |
| "silent musings of my heart" | Diary language | "the meditations of my heart" (the Psalm's own words) |
| "tune the heart's desire" | Soft and vague | "His statutes are the heart's desire" (accurate) |
| "dearer than" | Sentimental valuation | "worth far more than" (judgment of value) |
| "rise pleasing" | Soft | "ring true" (steel, a bell) |
| "healing Sun" | Soft emphasis (fine in Malachi, weak as a climax) | "righteous Sun" (Mal. 4:2, which goes on: "ye shall tread down the wicked") |

**Titles for God:** King, Lord, Rock, Strength, Redeemer, Judge, Commander,
Captain, Shield, Sword (Deut. 33:29), the Word, the Sun of righteousness.

**Titles for Christ in action:** the Son who came down, the one who bore my
curse and shame (Heb. 12:2), the one who kept the law and took its blows.

**Verbs for the man:** stand, hold, keep, guard, bar, strike, march, wield,
bear, fight, bow, swear. Use "run" only in the sense of a race or a course
(Heb. 12:1, Ps. 119:32), never as running *to* someone's arms.

**Keep Scripture's own images even when they sound tender.** Bridegroom, honey,
and the bruised reed all stay. Pair each with its strong counterpart as
Scripture does: bridegroom with strong man (Ps. 19:5); the bruised reed with
the might that won't break it (Isa. 42:3). Restraint by a strong man is
masculine.

**Don't overcorrect into bloodlust.** Wrath is "slow to wrath and swift to
hear", "not for any private wrong", and "does not bear the sword in vain".
Holy fury is always paired with reverent fear.

## Theology guardrails

The theology is Reformed and confessional (Westminster, Heidelberg), with
postmillennial hope.

- **Keep God's role and man's role distinct.** God does what only God does:
  Christ ends the devil's works (1 John 3:8) and the Lord slays the dragon
  (Isa. 27:1). The man *wars*, *stands*, and *holds*, but does not accomplish
  redemption. Test every line: "He lives to end the devil's reign" overclaims;
  "He wars until the dragon's slain" does not.
- **Satan does not reign.** He is defeated (Col. 2:15), even though he is still
  called "prince of this world". Christ reigns.
- **Christ fulfills the law. He is not "the Law".** "Perfect Law that turned my
  soul" addressed to Christ is a category error. "Voice that turned my wandering
  soul" is not.
- **Avoid phrasing that sounds like Christ sinned.** "Ran the sinner's race"
  suggests it; "ran my forfeit race" does not.
- **Grace, not merit.** The man stands "hidden in the Crucified", not in his own
  virtue. A song about virtues still roots them in Christ enthroned in the heart.
- **The song ends on God.** Make the last line a confession, doxology, or
  promise, not the singer's achievement.
- **No false assurance and no despair.** Death is gain (Phil. 1:21) and "if he
  falls, he falls to reign" (2 Tim. 2:12).

## Handling Scripture

- **Address God as "You / Your", capitalized, throughout.** Don't mix it with
  "Thou / Thy". The single exception is a verbatim KJV quotation, which keeps
  its own pronouns. If the modern form sounds awkward in a line ("Be You their
  Strength"), recast the line rather than switching to "Thou": for example,
  "Then be their Strength" or "LORD, be their Strength". Use "LORD" in capitals
  where the KJV has it (the divine Name). Elsewhere use "Lord".
- **Use the KJV** for wording and imagery. Keep its spellings: "yea" (never
  "yay"), "bruisèd", "hath" if used. Use British spellings when quoting the
  Book of Praise (the Heidelberg series).
- **The text's own image comes first.** When a psalm has already made the
  image, don't swap it for something cleverer (see honeycomb / lion's comb in
  `craft.md`). Tighten the phrasing, and keep the picture.
- **You don't have to set every image.** A song is not a translation. Keep
  the source's images that belong to the song's world, and drop the rest.
  Psalm 144's "great waters" did not belong in a song about shepherds and
  giants. When you keep an idiom, render what it *means*: "right hand of
  falsehood" means false oaths, and "touch the mountains" means a theophany.
  Don't keep phrasing that is odd in English, and don't keep a detail whose
  point the listener can't see.
- **Check narrative facts.** Lines about the Passion, David, and other
  biblical events must match the account. Christ was scourged, crowned with
  thorns, nailed, and pierced by a spear. He did not "feel the sword". A
  prophecy's figure of speech (Zech. 13:7, "Awake, O sword") is not a
  narrative detail.
- **Keep paraphrase accurate.** "Restores the soul", not "turns the soul to
  rest". "Statutes… rejoicing the heart", not "rule desire".
- **Cross-references are welcome** when they deepen a line and read clearly the
  first time. Some that worked well:
  - Acts 2 tongues of flame, for the day's speech
  - Heb. 4:12 two-edged sword, for the searching Word
  - Rev. 19:13–15, where the Word, the sword, and flesh meet
  - Ex. 17:12, Aaron holding up Moses' hands
  - Prov. 24:12, "I did not know"
  - Ps. 15:4, swearing to one's own hurt
  - Mark 3:27, binding the strong man and taking back the prey
- **Keep a citation list** of the references behind key images for the owner's
  liner notes.

## Brand lexicon

These fit the song register naturally:

- sworn
- hold the line
- stand
- the breach
- kingdom
- King of kings
- dominion
- no neutral ground
- guard the gate
- worthy
- Deus vult (used sparingly, as an occasional tag, never as filler)
